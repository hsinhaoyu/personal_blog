# Two postmoden novels about early moden science

In the 1994 novel [_The Island of the Day Before_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Island_of_the_Day_Before) by Umberto Eco, a modern editor studied fragemented manuscripts of uncertain origin (possibily recovered by [Abel Tasman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Tasman) in his 1642 expedition of the Southern Pacific) and attempted to reconstruct the history of a 17th century Italian nobleman stranded on a deserted ship on a mysterious mission. The nobelman was likely a spy of Cardinal Mazarin, the chief minister of Louis XIV, and might have seen a [quokka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quokka) before the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh landed on [Rottnest Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rottnest_Island) of moden Western Australia.

In another novel published in the same decade -  [_Mason & Dixon_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mason_%26_Dixon) by Thomas Pynchon - a clergyman of questionable denomination was forced to spend Chrismas in his sister's house in 18th-centry Philadelphia, and had to resort to telling tall tales to entertain unruly chilren (and sometimes adults). He did this by recounting the history of the two British astronomers who charted the boundary of Pennsylvania and Maryland in colonial America. The good father might or might not have been a member of Mason and Dixon's 1763-1767 landmark expedition and most certainly had fabricated a lot of his stories.

_The Island of the Day Before_ and _Mason & Dixon_ are both encyclopedic novels with a lot to say on a wide range of esoteric subjects, but a thread runs through them - the complex relationship between theoretical sciences and their practical applications. In particular, in the background of both novels is the 100+ years' history of European scientists' attempt to solve the _Problem of longitude_. It's an interesting coincidence (?) that in the same decade, Dava Sobel published a popular [non-fictional book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_(book)) on this subject. Why was there so much interest in the 1990s about the longitude?

### The romance of observational astronomy
Undenyably there is a romantic and viscral excitement associated with the obsevation of stars. The technicality, however, seems to have deterred novelists from depicting it in literature. What I find so interesting about _Mason & Dixon_ and _The Island_ is that they go into great details about the intricate apparatus and the complex procedures used.

In _The Island of the Day Before_, the protagonist assisted an eccentric Jesuit Father (modeled on [Gaspar Schott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspar_Schott)) in an observation of the eclipse of Jupiter's moons - about 30 years after their discovery by Galileo Galilei. Galileo himself invented an instrument called [Celatone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celatone) for making precise measurements of these events, but the apparatus described in the novel - _Instrumentum Arcetricum_ - is a Rube Goldberg contraption that reads like a parody of the complicated machines of the Baroque era.
