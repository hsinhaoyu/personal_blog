I have been listening to Balinese gamelan records. Not just any gamelan records. I was looking for a particular variety known as [gamelan semar pegulingan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan_semar_pegulingan).  The sound of semar pegulingan is delicate and gentle - it's like the cool jazz of gamelan compared to the aggressive [gong keybar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan_gong_kebyar) (the bebop of gamelan?).  I don't want it to be just pleasant sounds playing in the background, so I decided to dig into the historical context of these records.  What I've learned relates to some of the most dramatic moments in the history of Bali, so I think it's worthy to tell the stories here.

Semar pegulingan is _old_.  Prior to the Dutch colonization of Bali in the early 20th century, it was an essential part of the royal courts for hundreds of years.  In the late afternoons and evenings, it was the sweet sound of semar pegulingan that relaxed the minds of the royal family and allured them to sleep.  However, under Dutch administration, the palaces gradually lost their powers in the first decade of the 1900s, so the art declined.  The Canadian composer and musicologist Colin McPhee wrote in his memoir that in the 1930s, most of the royal semar pegulingan instruments had been melted down for other purposes, and it was difficult to find this music performed in the island.  We are very lucky that we can still find semar peggulingan albums that are connected to the past.

The word _gamelan_ can refer to the music, the musicians in an orchestra, and the instruments in an orchestra. In this article I'll talk a bit on the instruments, because in Balinese gamelan, instruments in an orchestra are built as an inseparable set. The tuning of each orchestra is different, so the instruments are as unique as the musicians that play them.  This is why when I listened to the 3 albums below, I naturally thought about the lineage of the instruments.


## The Bali 1928 recordings

{{< figure src="/images/WA-2015-cover-copy-1024x1024.jpg" alt="Bali 1928 Volume III album cover" width="400px" >}}

In 1928, European record companies made a series of 78 rpm recordings in Bali. Prior to WW2, this collection was the only Balinese gamelan records commercially available in the West. The [Bali 1928 Volume III: Lotring and the Sources of Gamelan Tradition](https://arbiterrecords.org/catalog/bali-1928-volume-iii-lotring-and-the-sources-of-gamelan-tradition/) album is a curated collection of this historical recordings. It contains 3 semar peggulingan pieces performed by musicians in Banjar Titih, Denpasar in 1928, when Bali was a Dutch colony.  The instruments, however, are much older.

The instruments belonged to Puri Pemecutan, which was one of the palaces of the Kingdom of Badung of pre-modern Bali.  In 1906, the Dutch launched military attacks on the kingdom as the first step towards annexing the entire island.  In defiance to the superior power of the Dutch, the rulers of the royal palaces led their subjects in _puputan_ - mass ritual suicide.  According to [an article](https://asia-archive.si.edu/essays/article-yamin/) by Tyler Yamin, the gamelan semar pegulingan of the Pemecutan palace was rescued from flames by court members.  However, the palace was in ruins and Dutch solders used the instruments for sports.  Afterwards, the instruments were pawned to Titih musicians, whose performance was captured on discs in 1928. These recordings are the only audio memories of this royal gamelan that witnessed the tragic event.

Gamelan semar pegulingan as an art form did not die with the Dutch colonisation. Instead, it evolved into a new style - gamelan palégongan. The Bali 1928 Volume III album also collects several tracks of this modernised semar pegulingan, so it's a good place to study the evolution.

## Gamelan Semar Puglingan Saih Pitu - The Heavenly Orchestra of Bali 

{{< figure src="/images/51outo-xTEL._UF894,1000_QL80_.jpg" alt="Bali 1928 Volume III album cover" width="400px" >}}

This album was recorded in Kamasan, Klungkung in 1991.  The instruments bear witness to _another_ tragedy.

Two years after the Badung pututan, the Dutch invaded the kingdom of Klungkung.  The Raja of Klungkung again led his entire court to their final act of defiance - another puputan.  The palace and the gamelan semar pugulingan were destroyed.  After more than a decade, the Kamasan village near the palace, wishing to reconnect with its past, reconstructed the royal gamelan semar pugulingan based on the villagers' memories of the sounds they heard from the other side of the palace wall.  Because gamelan compositions are orally transmission, a major obstacle was that nobody knew how to play the music because all the court musicians were thought to have died. Luckily, one of them  survived the slaughter and was able to teach a new generation of musicians to play the court repertoire.  Tyler Yamin's [article](https://asia-archive.si.edu/essays/article-yamin/) goes into more details about its history, but to make a long story short, only a small number of court compositions survived to this day, due to the chaos of WW2 and the early years of the Republic of Indonesia.  In this album, we get to hear 4 semar pegulingan compositions of the past.

What's so remarkable about this orchestra is that it remains active today.  Here is a performance of one of the tracks in this album at the odalan (temple festival) of Puro Bale Batur in 2024.

This is one of my favorite gamelan videos because it's interactive on Youtube. You can look around and observe the instruments and musicians (and their kids). Of particular interest is the _trompong_. It's the long row of gongs played by the musician facing the courtyard. The trompong is the defining instrument of semar pegulingan, which is used to play the main melody.

Another point of interest is that this set of instruments is _Saih Pitu_, meaning that it is tuned to the 7-tone pélog scale.  If you turn the camera to face the back of the room, you'll see that the instruments (_gangas_) all have 7 keys.  Seven-tone instruments are rare in modern gamelans, as most gamelans today are tuned to a 5-tone subset of the 7-tone pélog scale.

Most semar pegulingan music use only a 5-tone subset of the 7 tones.  In the video above, observe that the _ganga_ players always avoid 2 specific keys. In the video below, you can see that the musicians avoid a different set of two keys.  The music in based on a rare 5-tone scale (the _baro_ mode of the pélog scale), giving the piece a very exotic tonality.

## Bali: Gamelan Semar Pegulingan - Gamelan of the Love God
{{< figure src="/images/EXPLORER_SERIES_Bali.jpg" alt="Bali 1928 Volume III album cover" width="400px" >}}

Part of the Nonesuch Explorer Series, [this well-known album](https://www.nonesuch.com/albums/bali-gamelan-semar-pegulingan-gamelan-love-god) was recorded in the 1970s by [Robert E. Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Brown) at Teges Kanyinan, Pliatan.  We know quite a bit about this instrument set because they are described in detail in Colin McPhee's memoir _A House in Bali_. While he lived in a mountain village in Bali in the 1930s, McPhee petitioned to the community leaders to create a new semar pegulingan orchestra, in an attempt to preserve this dying art form.  Through his music teacher I Madé Lebah, McPhee was able to barrow a set of 600 year old instruments that belonged to Puri Pliatan (the Royal Palace of Pliatan)[^1]. The set was incomplete so McPhee commissioned the construction of a new trompong and other instruments.  He also invited an old man who used to be a court musician to his village to teach young musicians the semar pegulingan repertoire.  Unfortunately, WW2 broke out soon after so this instrument set went dormant in the turmoils of the Japanese occupation and the Indonesian Revolution.  It was not until the 1960s that I Madé Lebah brought it back to action.  

This album is an excellent showcase of Pliatan musicianship, but keep in mind that the music is played in a modern style. It's not the austere court music that we heard in the previous Kamasan album.  This can be readily appreciated in a composition titled _Tabuh Gari_, which is featured in all 3 albums. Tabuh Gari is an ancient composition from the [Gambuh](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KqAZFaELv4w&list=RDKqAZFaELv4w&start_radio=1) tradition of music drama. The Kamasan version strikingly does not have interlocked figurations (_kotekan_), while the other two versions are much more ornate and complex.  My favorite version is probably the Bali 1928 recording.

[^1]: Peliatan was not attacked by the Dutch because the kingdom of Gianyar ceded its sovereignty to the Netherlands in 1900.  The royal place, however, was unable to maintain a semar pegulingan orchestra, so the instruments were loan to a legong dance club.
